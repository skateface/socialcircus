Most scavenger hunts don't fail because the clues are too easy or too hard. They fail because the host didn't decide, in advance, how a team actually finishes — so half the group is still hunting for item #7 while the other half has been standing around for twenty minutes. Get the format, the clue list, and the finish line settled before anyone starts, and the hunt runs itself.

## 1. Pick your format before you write a single clue

There are three formats, and they solve different logistics problems:

- **Photo hunt** — teams take a photo completing each task or finding each item, then text or upload it. No physical collection, easy to verify, and it works indoors, outdoors, or split across both. This is the right default for almost every group.
- **Physical-item hunt** — teams collect actual objects and bring them back to a home base. More satisfying to see a pile of "finds" at the end, but harder to run for anything valuable or fragile, and someone has to sort through everyone's loot afterward.
- **Clue-chain hunt** — each clue's answer points to the location of the next clue, ending at a final prize or reveal. Highest payoff format, but it only works if every clue is solvable and every hiding spot is actually accessible — one bad clue and the whole chain stalls.

For a first-time host, or any group larger than 15, go with a photo hunt. It scales cleanly and never leaves you refereeing whether an item "counts."

## 2. Decide the boundaries before you write the list

Before writing a single item, nail down three things that determine how the whole hunt will feel:

1. **The area.** A single building, a few blocks of a neighborhood, or an entire city — pick one and state it explicitly. An unbounded hunt ("anywhere in town") sounds fun but usually means teams spend more time driving than hunting.
2. **The time limit.** Announce it up front as a hard stop, not a suggestion — "back at home base by 60 minutes" gives every team the same clock to plan around.
3. **Rules of engagement.** Can teams split up? Is driving allowed, or is it walking-only? Can they ask strangers for help? Decide these before launch — mid-hunt is the wrong time to settle a dispute over whether a team cheated.

## 3. Build a list that's hard but fair

A scavenger hunt list lives or dies on variety. A list of 15-20 items or tasks works well for a 60-90 minute hunt, mixed across a few types so no single skill set dominates:

- **Find it** — locate a specific object, landmark, or sign (a red mailbox, a business with a certain name, a statue).
- **Do it** — complete a small task or challenge (get a stranger to give you a high five, do 10 jumping jacks in front of a specific store).
- **Solve it** — a riddle or puzzle whose answer is a location or object, not just handed to them directly.
- **Get it** — a photo with a specific person, group, or in a specific pose.
- **Make it** — build or create something small out of found materials (a tower, a piece of art from trash).

A useful gut check while writing: if you can't picture a specific way a team would actually accomplish an item, rewrite it. Vague items ("something that represents summer") produce vague, low-effort submissions and slow the hunt down while teams argue about what counts.

## 4. Set team size and assign a role or two

Teams of 3-5 work best — big enough to split up tasks, small enough that nobody's just along for the ride. Once a team's size is set, suggest (don't force) a couple of informal roles:

- **A navigator**, keeping track of the map or route.
- **A photographer**, responsible for actually capturing and sending each submission.
- **A reader**, who keeps the list and reads the next item out loud before the team moves.

You don't need to assign these formally — most teams sort themselves out in the first five minutes. But mentioning it during the kickoff briefing heads off the common failure mode where everyone crowds around one phone arguing about the same clue.

## 5. Structure the timeline so it doesn't drag or rush

For a 90-minute hunt with a group of 15-30 people, a workable structure looks like:

- **0:00-0:10** — Kickoff. Explain the format, hand out the list, confirm boundaries and the hard stop time, form teams.
- **0:10-0:70** — The hunt itself. If you're running a photo hunt, this is a good window to check submissions as they come in rather than waiting until the end.
- **0:70-0:80** — Buffer. Teams straggle back; this is normal, and building in ten minutes of slack means you're not starting the reveal while people are still finishing.
- **0:80-0:90** — Scoring and reveal.

If you're running this as part of a longer event rather than the whole night, keep the hunt itself to 45-60 minutes and lean on our <a href="/blog/how-to-host-a-game-night/">game night guide</a> for how to fill the time before and after.

## 6. Score it so a slow start isn't a dead end

Flat points-per-item is the simplest system, but a team that falls behind early has no way back — and a team that knows it's lost tends to stop trying, which drags down the whole finish. Two easy fixes:

- **Weight the harder items.** A riddle-solve or "do it" challenge should be worth more than a simple "find it" — it rewards effort without requiring a team to sweep every single item.
- **Add a bonus item revealed halfway through.** A single high-value surprise task, texted out at the 30-minute mark, gives every team — leading or trailing — a reason to keep pushing.

## 7. A few mistakes that sink most first-timers

- **No hard stop time.** Without one, teams keep hunting past the point where you're ready to score, and the reveal gets awkward and staggered instead of a single fun moment.
- **Items that depend on luck, not effort.** A clue that only works if a specific store happens to be open, or a specific stranger happens to be around, will tank one team through no fault of their own. Test every location-dependent item yourself beforehand.
- **No way to verify a submission.** Decide up front how disputes get settled ("host's call is final, no debate") so you're not negotiating over a blurry photo while everyone else waits.
- **Skipping a test run of the route.** If your hunt spans multiple locations, walk or drive it yourself first — a route that looks fine on a map can turn out to have a closed shortcut or a much longer walk than expected.
