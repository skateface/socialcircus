Friendsgiving fails in one of two ways: either everyone shows up with a side dish and nobody brought a turkey, or the host tries to cook the whole spread solo and spends the entire party sweating in the kitchen instead of sitting at the table. Both are fixable with the same fix — decide the format, assign the dishes, and time the day before you send a single invite.

## 1. Pick your format before you send invites

Friendsgiving isn't real Thanksgiving, and it doesn't need to follow the same rules. Three formats work, and which one fits depends on your group size and how much cooking anyone actually wants to do:

- **Full potluck** — the host provides the space, the plates, and maybe the turkey; everyone else signs up for a side, a dessert, or a drink. This is the right default for groups of 8 or more — it splits the labor and the cost, and it's the format most guests already expect from a Friendsgiving invite.
- **Host cooks, guests bring extras** — the host handles the main and one or two anchor sides, guests bring smaller additions (a dessert, a bottle of wine, an appetizer). Works well for smaller, closer groups of 4-6 where one person genuinely wants to cook the meal.
- **Order-in Friendsgiving** — skip the cooking almost entirely and go with a catered spread or a mix of takeout. Nobody's first choice, but it's a legitimate option for a group that wants the gathering without the kitchen marathon, especially on a tight timeline.

For a first-time host, full potluck is the safest call. It's the format that scales best, and it means no single person is responsible for the meal actually landing on time.

## 2. Build the guest list and lock the date early

Friendsgiving competes with real Thanksgiving travel, in-law obligations, and other friends' Friendsgivings — so the date matters more here than for most parties.

1. **Pick a weekend before or after actual Thanksgiving**, not the same day. The two weekends flanking it are the most common choice, since guests can commit without worrying about double-booking their actual holiday.
2. **Send invites 3-4 weeks out.** Any later and you're building your final headcount and menu around whoever happens to still be free.
3. **Attach the dish sign-up to the invite itself**, not a follow-up message. A shared note, a group chat poll, or a simple spreadsheet link all work — the point is that a guest can claim a dish the moment they RSVP, before someone else takes the one they wanted to make.

## 3. Plan the menu so it isn't six pans of mashed potatoes

Left to their own devices, a group of well-meaning guests will produce four different mac and cheeses and no vegetables. Structure the sign-up around categories instead of a blank slate:

- **One or two mains** — turkey, a ham, or a vegetarian centerpiece (a stuffed squash or a hearty casserole work well as the plant-based anchor).
- **Starches** — mashed or roasted potatoes, stuffing, mac and cheese. Cap this category at two dishes; it's the one that always overflows.
- **Vegetables** — green beans, roasted brussels sprouts, a salad. This is the category that gets skipped if you don't explicitly ask for it.
- **Bread** — rolls or cornbread. Low effort, always disappears.
- **Desserts** — pie is the expected default, but leave room for one wildcard.
- **Drinks** — wine, a batch cocktail, or cider. One person bringing "drinks" as a catch-all is fine for groups under 10; past that, split it into two assignments so nobody's covering the whole bar alone.

Once a category fills up, close it in the sign-up and steer the next volunteer toward whatever's still empty. It's a lot easier to redirect someone before they've bought ingredients than after.

## 4. Time the day so cooking doesn't eat the whole party

The single biggest Friendsgiving mistake is setting one arrival time and expecting food on the table immediately. Reheating a dozen dishes from a dozen kitchens takes real time, and a hungry, unfed group standing around for 45 minutes sours the whole afternoon. Instead:

- **Set arrival 60-90 minutes before you plan to eat.** This gives you a buffer to reheat, plate, and deal with the inevitable dish that shows up needing 20 more minutes in the oven.
- **Ask guests to note reheating needs when they sign up**, not when they walk in. Knowing in advance that two dishes need oven time and one needs a stovetop lets you actually plan the sequence instead of triaging in real time.
- **Build in a buffer, not a hard start time**, for the meal itself. "Food around 3:00" survives a dish running late; "dinner at 3:00 sharp" doesn't.
- **Stagger dessert.** Don't put pie on the table at the same time as the mains — let there be a natural lull between dinner and dessert so people aren't stuffed and rushing through the best part.

## 5. Keep people entertained while the food's in the oven

That 60-90 minute arrival window is dead air if you don't plan for it — and it's the best window in the whole day to get people talking instead of just standing near the kitchen watching the host work.

## 6. Handle dietary needs and drinks without a spreadsheet

Ask about dietary restrictions on the same invite where you assign dishes, not as a separate follow-up. Two things make this painless:

- **Ask everyone, not just the people you suspect have a restriction.** A single question on the invite ("any allergies or dietary needs?") normalizes it and means you're not singling anyone out.
- **Assign at least one dish in every major category (main, starch, vegetable, dessert) to be free of the most common restrictions** — gluten, dairy, meat. It's a lot easier to guarantee one solidly safe option per category than to modify every dish on the table.

## 7. A few mistakes that sink most first-timers

- **No dish sign-up, just a vague "bring something."** This is how you end up with three desserts and zero vegetables. Assign categories, not vibes.
- **One arrival time doubling as the meal time.** Build in the reheating buffer described above, or you'll have a hungry, restless group for the first half hour.
- **Not planning for leftovers.** Friendsgiving generates a genuinely absurd amount of food. Have containers on hand and send people home with plates — it's the easiest way to make sure the host isn't stuck with six pies.
- **Forgetting the day is also a hangout, not just a meal.** The best Friendsgivings build in time before and after the food, not just the meal itself — treat it like the game night guide below, not a formal dinner you rush through.
